What Actually Fails Inside a Faucet

Modern single-handle faucets run on cartridges — a self-contained cylinder that mixes hot and cold and controls flow. When the seals inside wear out, you get drips, stiff handles, or sudden temperature swings. Two-handle faucets use compression stems with rubber washers that flatten over time; cranking the handle harder only buys a week and gouges the valve seat underneath. O-rings dry out and crack, especially on swiveling spouts. Valve seats corrode right where the washer presses, and no new washer will seal against a pitted seat until it's resurfaced. Aerators clog with sediment and mineral scale, which feels like low water pressure but is really just a blocked screen. Redmond's moderately hard water accelerates all of this — dissolved minerals grind away at seals a little more with every use. None of these parts are expensive. The real skill is diagnosing which one failed, because a drip at the spout and a leak at the handle base are different problems with different fixes.

Our Repair-vs-Replace Decision Rule

Here is the rule we apply on every call. Repair the fixture when it's a quality brand under about fifteen years old, the problem is isolated to internal parts, the finish still looks good, and replacement parts are actually available. A forty-dollar cartridge in a solid faucet is one of the best values in all of plumbing. Replace the fixture when the body itself is cracked or corroded, parts are obsolete (we see this constantly with 1980s off-brand faucets), the same fixture has failed twice in two years, or the finish is so worn you're embarrassed when guests visit. There is also an efficiency angle worth doing the math on: a pre-1994 faucet can flow at three gallons per minute or more, while a modern WaterSense model uses 1.5 or less without feeling weaker in daily use. If your fixture is old enough to vote, replacement often pays for itself in water savings within a couple of years. We'll quote both options so you decide with real figures.

The Fixtures We Repair Most in Redmond

Kitchen pull-down faucets top our list: the cartridge wears, the retractable hose frays where it rubs, and the spray head clogs with scale. Bathroom widespread faucets come next, usually stem washers on the hot side, because hot water kills rubber faster than cold. Shower valves are a specialty of ours — a failing pressure-balance cartridge is the classic cause of "the shower goes scalding when someone flushes a toilet," and it's a thirty-minute fix once properly diagnosed. Hose bibbs crack from freeze damage, and we see a wave of them every spring after the first garden hose gets connected to a bibb whose interior pipe split over winter. Toilet fill and flush valves aren't faucets, but they're fixtures we rebuild constantly — a running toilet is usually a fifteen-dollar flapper or a worn fill valve, not a reason to replace the whole toilet.

When Replacement Is the Smarter Money

Sometimes the honest advice is a new fixture. If yours is over twenty years old, shows corrosion inside the body, or needs its third repair, you're renting a problem instead of owning a solution. Very old brass fixtures can also predate modern lead-free standards — one more reason we recommend replacement on the oldest ones we encounter.
